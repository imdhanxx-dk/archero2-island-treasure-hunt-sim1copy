# III. NOCTRA — THE KEEPER OF THE INTERVAL

## The office that was deleted

Before there was a Starkeeper, there were **two** keepers.

One held the lights.

The other held **the space between them**.

The formal title was *Keeper of the Interval.* Everyone called it Nightkeeper.
The two offices were founded in the same season, by the same council, for a
reason the founders considered so obvious it is recorded in a single line and
never elaborated:

> *A constellation is not made of stars. It is made of stars and the distance
> between them. Someone must keep the distance.*

The Nightkeeper's duties were unglamorous and constant. Maintain the dark
galleries where Eclipse-silk is spun. Weave each Starkeeper's scarf. Walk the
boundary hours — dusk and dawn — and keep them honest. Protect the things that
only happen when no one is looking: the opening of night-flowers, the crossing
of migrating birds that steer by stars and cannot steer by anything else, the
hatching seasons, the rest of every creature that needs the world to stop
displaying itself for a few hours so that sleep can occur.

Veylora understood this once. Night was not the enemy of life. Night was **half
of the machine**.

Then the lamps came.

---

## THE LONG BRIGHT

It began, as these things do, as an unambiguous good.

Mirrowen lit its first street. Then its first district. Then the bridges, so
that no one would fall. Then the harbour, so that no ship would founder. Then
the orchards, so that the harvest could run past sundown. Then every street,
every tower, every span, every window — because why would you *not*? Light was
safety. Light was industry. Light was the visible, glowing proof that Veylora
was no longer afraid of anything.

Within two generations there was no true darkness within forty miles of the
capital, and the maps of the era show the glow of Mirrowen drawn as a feature
of the landscape, like a mountain.

The failures were not dramatic. That is the important part. Nothing exploded.

**The Bellrose stopped opening.** A pale flower that unfurls only in true dark,
the sole pollen source of a moth that pollinates a third of the eastern
orchards. The Bellrose did not die. It simply sat closed, in a world that was
never dark enough, waiting for a night that no longer came.

**The moths went to the lamps.** All of them. Every night. They circled until
they dropped, and in the morning the lamplighters swept them off the glass in
drifts, and everyone agreed it was a shame, and nobody connected it to the
orchards for eleven years.

**The glass-turtles of Crystal Coast crawled the wrong way.** Hatchlings find
the sea by running toward the brightest horizon, which for the entire history of
the species has been the ocean under an open sky. Mirrowen was brighter. Three
consecutive generations of hatchlings walked inland, into the city, and died on
warm flagstones under beautiful lanterns.

**The Nightbirds stopped arriving.** They navigated by the turning of the sky —
the star compass, the still point. Above the capital there was no longer any
sky to turn. The flocks scattered, circled, exhausted themselves against lit
towers. The great autumn crossing that had darkened Veylora's sky for a
thousand years thinned to nothing in under a century, and the last reliable
account of it is a child's drawing.

**And people stopped sleeping properly,** which is the one that finally got
someone's attention, because it happened to *them*.

Noctra brought all of it to the council. Every figure. Every count. The moth
drifts, the turtle tracks, the empty sky in autumn, the orchards yielding less
each year. He was the Keeper of the Interval and he had the whole grim ledger,
and he laid it out and he told them, plainly, what it added up to:

**The lamps are killing the night, and the night is load-bearing.**

The council heard him out.

And then it drew the opposite conclusion.

Because there was another explanation available, and it was so much easier. Look
at the facts, they said. Crops failing. Creatures dying. Sickness in the cities.
Children afraid to sleep. Something is wrong with Veylora — and here is a man
who has spent his life in the dark, who has a *star of darkness* in his keeping,
who speaks for the black galleries under the earth.

*Something is wrong, and it is coming from the dark, and you are the dark's
representative.*

It is the oldest error there is. The dark did not do it. The dark was simply
the last place anyone looked and the easiest thing to blame, because it is the
only part of the world that cannot defend itself by being examined.

> *"They were standing in the wreckage of what the light had done, holding
> lanterns, and they asked me what the darkness wanted from them."*
> — Noctra, four hundred years later, still not over it

## THE FIRST ECLIPSE

History says Noctra fought Veylora and lost.

He did not fight. There was no battle. There is not one account of a battle
because there was not one, and the absence should have made someone suspicious
centuries ago.

**There was a vote.**

Twelve council members, in a warm room, well lit. The motion was not
imprisonment. The motion was far worse, and Noctra had known it was coming for
a year.

**The motion was to unmake the Shadow Star.**

Not seal. Not hide. *End.* Remove the verb WITHHOLD from the Tenfold Sky
permanently, on the grounds that Veylora no longer required it and had
demonstrably suffered from it. Nine regions, they reasoned. A cleaner number.
And then, forever, a world with nothing in it that could not be seen.

Noctra understood two things that the room did not.

The first: **it would have worked.** Not the intent — the mechanism. You cannot
delete a force from the world, but a Convocation does not need to delete it.
A Convocation only needs the world to *agree* that it was never there, and the
difference between those is invisible from inside. Veylora would have woken up
in a world with no rest in it, no concealment, no contrast, nothing for a light
to be distinguished against — and it would have had no words left to describe
what was missing, and it would have called the resulting horror *progress*.

The second: **there was one alternative, and it cost exactly one person.**

A Star cannot be destroyed if it is already **bound inside something that
refuses to release it.** A sealed thing survives. An unmade thing does not.

So the Keeper of the Interval stood up in a warm, well-lit room and offered
the council a compromise they were delighted to accept, because it gave them
everything they wanted and let them feel merciful about it.

**"Bind it in me. Put us both under the Sanctuary. You will never see either of
us again, and you will not have to explain to your grandchildren why the world
stopped having a night in it."**

They took the deal in under an hour.

The binding required a Starkeeper. There was one Starkeeper.

**Aurelle did it.** His partner of thirty years, the woman who had woven
nothing in her life because he wove for her, the only creature in Veylora who
knew exactly what he was giving up and exactly why.

She argued for four days. She lost, because he was right.

Then she performed the binding herself, because if it was going to be done she
was not going to let it be done badly, and the last thing Noctra saw before four
hundred years of dark was Aurelle's face doing something he has never been able
to describe.

**And then she wrote the history.**

This is the part that cannot be forgiven and also cannot be condemned, and the
story should never let anyone off the hook for it.

She had a problem. A sealed Star is only safe while nobody goes looking for it.
And a world that remembered a noble Nightkeeper who sacrificed himself would
*absolutely* go looking — out of guilt, out of reverence, out of ambition, out
of the simple human inability to leave a sealed door alone.

So she made the door unattractive.

She wrote him as a monster. She let the council scrub the statues, and she did
not stop them, and in some accounts she helped. She turned the Keeper of the
Interval into *Noctra, the Shadow Cat, who walks where the lanterns end* — a
story for frightening kittens into coming home before dark.

It worked perfectly. Nobody went looking. For four hundred years.

She meant it as a lock.

It became a **verdict**.

She died about sixty years later, having never once written the truth down
anywhere, because writing it down would have defeated the point. And so the only
copy of what actually happened at the First Eclipse went into the ground with
her, and the only other creature who knew was sealed under a mountain, and the
lie she built to protect him became the thing that made him into what he is now.

## FOUR HUNDRED YEARS

Do not imagine sleep. He was awake for all of it.

He did three things.

**He counted.** Days, at first. Then the drip in the eastern gallery. Then, for
about eighty years, nothing at all, which is the part he will not discuss.

**He built a palace.** Not of stone — of memory. With nothing to see, he
constructed an interior architecture and hung his life on it, room by room, so
that he would still have a self at the end. It is the exact technique the
Nightweave uses. He invented it for the same reason Keepers use it. He is, in
the most literal sense, the only creature in Veylora who has done to himself
what the scarf does to Lumi.

He is very, very good at it. It is why he is still coherent.

It is also why he is wrong about almost everything.

**He remembered the vote.** Over and over. Every day, for four centuries.

And here is the quiet horror at the centre of Noctra, the thing that makes him
tragic instead of merely angry:

**A memory is not a stone. It is a thing that gets rebuilt every time you pick
it up.** Every retrieval is a reconstruction, and every reconstruction is an
opportunity to be slightly wrong, and the wrongness compounds, and the memory
you have handled ten thousand times is the memory you can trust least.

He has taken the First Eclipse out and turned it over every single day for four
hundred years. He has worn it smooth. He has worn *grooves* in it.

He is no longer certain Aurelle argued for four days.

He is no longer certain she wept.

Some days he is fairly sure she read the binding from a page without looking up,
and he knows — intellectually, in the part of him that is still a scholar — that
this is almost certainly something his own mind manufactured to make the grief
survivable by converting it into rage.

But he cannot get back to the original. There is no original. There is only the
four-hundredth copy.

**This is the real reason he wants the Stars.**

Not power. Not revenge, though he would take it.

A Convocation restores the record. The *actual* record. The world's own memory,
underneath everyone's.

He needs to know whether she cried.

---

## WHAT HE WANTS, AND WHY IT IS STILL MONSTROUS

State his goal in plain terms and it is difficult to argue with:

*Restore the true history of the First Eclipse. Return Shadow to the Tenfold
Sky. Put the night back into the grammar of the world before the Long Bright
finishes what it started. Have his name exist again.*

Every one of those is **just**. Several are *urgent* — the orchards are still
failing, four hundred years on, and nobody in Mirrowen can say why.

The method is the crime.

Because the Stars will not consent to a Convocation. They have said so, loudly,
by leaving. And a Convocation that proceeds without consent is not a
restoration of the record —

— it is **the Redaction, run again, in the other direction.**

Every mind in Veylora rewritten. Every creature who currently believes Noctra
was a monster edited, without their knowledge or permission, into someone who
believes otherwise. Millions of people who would never learn they had been
changed, because there is no detectable difference between the past and the
record of the past.

He would be *right*. And he would be doing, to an entire world, the precise
thing that was done to him.

He knows this.

**That is the point.** He is not deluded about it, and the story must never let
him be. He worked it out roughly two hundred years in, and concluded that it was
worth it, and the two hundred years since have been spent maintaining that
conclusion against the part of himself that keeps quietly objecting.

He is not a villain who has miscalculated.

He is a good person who has been alone with an injustice for so long that he has
started to believe the injustice is the only real thing, and everything else —
everyone else, their memories, their consent, their small lit lives — is
scenery.

---

## THE GEM

The fractured violet stone in his chest is not an artefact of power. It is not
armour, not a focus, not a heart.

**It is the seal.** It is the Shadow Star itself, bound into him, exactly as he
asked for.

Four centuries of pressure have cracked it. The violet lines running through
his body are not corruption — they are the seal *leaking*, the bound verb
seeping out into the creature holding it, which is what the council was told
would not happen and which Noctra knew perfectly well would.

Which means the thing everyone in Veylora calls his monstrousness — the fur
that eats light, the smoke, the violet cracks — is not what he became.

It is what **carrying it for them** did to him.

And the single gold fracture that appears across the gem in the Star Vault, the
instant a small white cat says *that doesn't mean you're supposed to die* —

— that is not a wound.

**That is the seal loosening.**

Because a seal that was entered into voluntarily can only be released by the
same thing that closed it: someone deciding, freely, that he does not have to
stay in there.

Nobody has said anything like that to him in four hundred years.

He had honestly stopped expecting it.

And the worst part — the part that makes him roar and tear the Vault apart
rather than stand there one more second — is that he recognised the voice.
